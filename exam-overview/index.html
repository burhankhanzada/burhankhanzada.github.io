What the exam actually asks

Eight past papers taken apart, and one real 14-point task worked end to end.

Learning objectives

By the end of this section you will be able to:

  • Know the shape of the paper before you sit it
  • Recognise the handful of task types that make up every paper
  • Work a real task in the order the marks fall

The paper

Ninety minutes, and the shape of what you get changed with the most recent paper. The seven papers from September 2022 to September 2025 are four or five written tasks, between 40 and 49 points, with a formula help-section printed at the end: the PT₁ and PT₂ standard forms, the closed-loop formulas, a short table of Laplace correspondences and the element laws.

The paper of 17 February 2026 is not that. Six tasks, a hundred points, and no help-section at all — the only thing printed at the back is the construction of the Routh array. Here is the whole of it, and every task is a lesson in this part:

Read that table as a budget. Ninety minutes over a hundred points is a little under a minute a point, so the thermal task is worth about twenty-three minutes and the pole plots about eight. Two tasks carry half the paper and the other four carry the rest — and 42 of the hundred points are in formats no earlier paper used at all.

The missing help-section is the single biggest change, and it is easy to miss. Seven papers in a row printed the standard forms and a transform table, which made "which formula" the question and "do you know it" a non-question. The newest paper prints neither. The step response of a PT₂, the peak time, the closed-loop formula, the correspondences — on this paper they come from you. The formula sheet is the set worth having cold, and it is now worth having cold rather than worth recognising.
Two rules printed on the 2025 paper, worth reading twice. "The solution must be clearly presented. The sole indication of the result or answers without justification cannot be evaluated." — a bare correct number scores nothing, and shown working with a wrong number in it scores most of the marks. The 2026 paper says the same thing in its own words and then builds a whole task out of it, where the tick is worthless without the sentence beside it.
And the older shape, for the seven papers that still have it. The 27.09.2022 paper was 40 points over four tasks and a bonus: a five-point block-diagram reduction, a fourteen-point nonlinear mechanical system, a nine-point pressure cooker described in words, a twelve-point Routh and synthesis task, and three bonus points for a Padé approximation. Same material, fewer and larger pieces.

Read the right-hand column honestly. Every row now has a lesson behind it — but modelling is the door into roughly half the marks, and it is almost never the whole task. Every task that begins with a physical system carries on into Laplace, loops, stability or a controller, so the rows near the top of this table are the ones a modelling-only revision plan leaves you short on.

What the modelling half buys you is that you can start every one of those tasks. A task you cannot begin is worth nothing; a task you begin correctly and cannot finish is usually worth over half.

Look at the shape before the content. Five sub-questions, each one standing on the answer above it — and the first two are pure modelling. That is the pattern in every paper: the marks start where you already are, and walk away from it.

The equation itself is a mass on a spring, drawn sideways: a mass m{\htmlClass{mc1}{m}}m, viscous friction η{\htmlClass{mc5}{\eta}}η, and two springs of stiffness k{\htmlClass{mc2}{k}}k pulling from anchors a distance a{\htmlClass{mc2}{a}}a off the line of travel, each with natural length l{\htmlClass{mc2}{l}}l. You do not need that picture to answer — but it is where the square root comes from.

Do this one on paper first. Give it thirty minutes, then come back and step through the cards. Reading a solution you have not attempted feels like understanding and is not.
Three phrases in 2.1, and each one is an instruction. The wording of an exam question is not decoration — read it the way you would read a spec.
"stationary solutions"a fixed technical term, not "solve the ODE". Every derivative goes to zero. Also printed as equilibria, operating points, steady state or Ruhelagen "for u≡0{\htmlClass{mc4}{u}} \equiv 0u≡0"the input is given to you, so substitute it. The triple bar is "identically zero, for all time", not zero at one instant "depending on the parameters"a case distinction is being asked for. One formula is not an answer to this sentence, however right the formula is
The third is the one that costs marks, and it costs them silently: the algebra is correct, the examiner is looking for rows, and there is one line on the page.

How to attack one

The order that gets the most marks out of a task like that, in the time you have:

Read all sub-questions first

they tell you where the task is going, and 2.4 often reveals what 2.2 was for

Write the target form down before starting

Bank the modelling marks

equilibria, linearisation, order: the early sub-questions, and the ones you can do cold

The one habit worth more than any formula: when a sub-question stalls, write down what you would do if you had the previous answer, and do it with a symbol. The marks are for the method, and a paper with every part attempted symbolically beats a paper with two parts perfect and three blank.

Where the rest of a task is taught. The half of a paper that comes after G(s){\htmlClass{mc8}{G}}(s)G(s) has its own lessons: reducing a block diagram, Routh, the error that is left when it stops, and choosing the controller numbers. They all begin with a G(s){\htmlClass{mc8}{G}}(s)G(s) — and getting a physical system to a G(s){\htmlClass{mc8}{G}}(s)G(s) is the part you have just practised.
