The six moves

Every model in this app, and every modelling question in your exam papers, is these six moves in this order. Each one links to the lesson that teaches it.

  1. 1

    Draw the boundary. Name the input and the output.

    Ask: what can I change from outside, and what do I want to know? Write "in: __ out: __" before touching any physics — What a model actually is.
  2. 2

    Count the energy stores.

    Ask: if I froze time, what would still be holding something? The count is the order — Count the stores, know the order.
  3. 3

    Write one element law per component.

    Ask: what does this part do on its own? One law per component, checked at its extremes — the seven worth knowing cold are in Where the equations come from.
  4. 4

    Write one balance law per store.

    Ask: what has to add up? Rate stored = in − out, one balance per store — Where the equations come from and One balance, every domain.
  5. 5

    Assemble, then check.

    Ask: units, steady state, extremes, dots against stores, signs — one minute, and it catches nearly everything: Is my model wrong?
  6. 6

    Interpret before you solve.

    Ask: what is the gain, what is the time constant, can it overshoot? Read a first-order model in The water tank and a second-order one in Mass, spring and damper.
If you are stuck on a problem, you have almost certainly skipped a move. Go back to the earliest one you cannot answer confidently and start there.

solving an inequality, and the one move that flips it

Half the answers this course asks for are ranges rather than numbers — the gains that keep a loop stable, the parameters for which a solution exists at all — and a range comes out of an inequality. Every move you know from equations still works on one: add to both sides, subtract from both sides, multiply, divide. There is exactly one exception, and it is where the marks go.

Multiply or divide by a negative number and the sign turns round. Check it on numbers you can see: 3>13 > 13>1 is true, and multiplying both sides by −1-1−1 gives −3-3−3 and −1-1−1 — and −3-3−3 is less than −1-1−1. Adding and subtracting never do this; only the two moves that can change a sign do.

The loop

The six signals and two blocks every page from “Orientation” onwards assumes. These are the ones the exam paper prints in German.

setpoint W{\htmlClass{mc3}{W}}W The value you WANT the output to be.
The number on the thermostat dial. German: Führungsgröße. Sometimes written R(s) instead of W(s).

Units: same as the output

controlled variable Y{\htmlClass{mc2}{Y}}Y The thing you actually care about and are measuring.
Room temperature, water level, drone altitude. German: Regelgröße.

Units: depends

manipulated variable U{\htmlClass{mc4}{U}}U The knob the controller turns.
Valve opening, heater power, motor voltage. German: Stellgröße. It is the controller OUTPUT, not its input — a common mix-up.

Units: depends

disturbance Z{\htmlClass{mc5}{Z}}Z Anything outside the loop that pushes the output around.
Cold draught, someone opening a tap, wind on a drone. German: Störgröße. Rejecting these is the main reason feedback exists.

Units: depends

control error E{\htmlClass{mc6}{E}}E Setpoint minus measured value. How wrong you currently are.
E = W − Y. The controller works on this number. German: Regeldifferenz.

Units: same as the output

controller GR{\htmlClass{mc1}{G}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st7}{R}}GR​ The only block in the loop you get to design. It reads the error and decides what to do about it.
German: Regler — where the R comes from. It takes E = W − Y and produces U. A P controller multiplies by KR; a PI adds an integrator and kills the leftover offset; a PID adds a derivative term as well. Some books write GC for C-ontroller.
plant GS{\htmlClass{mc1}{G}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st3}{S}}GS​ The physical thing you are trying to control and cannot change.
The tank, the oven, the motor, the drone. German: Regelstrecke — which is where the S comes from, and why every card in this course writes GS. Many English books write GP for P-lant instead; same block. You design the controller around it; you do not get to redesign it.
open loop No measurement, no feedback. The controller acts blind.
A toaster on a timer. Cheap and can never destabilise anything, but any disturbance goes straight through to the output uncorrected. German: Steuerung.
closed loop The output is measured and fed back, so the controller reacts to what actually happened.
A thermostat. Corrects for disturbances it was never told about, but a bad tuning can make a perfectly stable plant unstable. German: Regelung.

Controller parameters

Note that T_N and T_V run in opposite directions — one is in a denominator and the other multiplies s.

KR{\htmlClass{mc8}{K}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st7}{R}}KR​ (controller gain) How hard the controller pushes for a given error.
Bigger KR = faster and less steady-state error, but more overshoot and eventually instability. English texts call it Kₚ.

Units: depends

TN{\htmlClass{mc1}{T}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st8}{N}}TN​ (reset time) Sets how strong the integral action is. SMALL T_N = STRONG I action.
It appears as 1/(TNs), so it is in the denominator — that is why small means strong. English texts call it Tᵢ (integral time). Getting this backwards is a classic exam slip.

Units: seconds

TV{\htmlClass{mc1}{T}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st9}{V}}TV​ (rate time) Sets how strong the derivative action is. BIG T_V = STRONG D action.
It appears as TVs, multiplying s directly — so bigger means stronger. The opposite convention to TN. English texts call it Tᵏ.

Units: seconds

PID controller Three actions added together: P reacts to now, I to the past, D predicts the future.
C(s) = KR(1 + 1/(TNs) + TVs). P makes it fast, I removes leftover error, D adds damping back. The industrial default for a reason.

Say it: pee-eye-dee

system type How many integrators (poles at s = 0) are in the open loop.
Type 0 leaves an error on a step. Type 1 tracks a step perfectly but lags a ramp. Type 2 tracks a ramp perfectly. Each integrator kills one order of input.
steady-state error e∞e\inftye∞ How far off the target you still are after everything has settled down.
A P controller always leaves some. Adding an integrator (the I in PI or PID) drives it to exactly zero, because the integrator keeps accumulating until nothing is left.

Units: same as the output

The s-domain

Where a differential equation goes to become algebra.

s The Laplace variable. Think of it as a stand-in for "d/dt".
The whole point: differentiating in time becomes MULTIPLYING by s. So a differential equation turns into ordinary algebra you can rearrange. s is a complex number, which is why poles can have imaginary parts.

Units: per second

Say it: ess

Laplace transform A trick that turns calculus into algebra.
You start with a differential equation (hard). You apply the transform. Every d/dt becomes an s (easy). You do simple algebra. You look up the answer in a table to come back to time. That is the entire method.

Say it: la-PLAHSS

transfer function G(s){\htmlClass{mc1}{G}}(s)G(s) Output divided by input, in the s-world. It fully describes how a system responds.
G(s) = Y(s)/U(s). Once you have it you can predict everything: speed, overshoot, stability. It only exists for LINEAR systems with zero starting conditions.

Units: depends

pole A value of s that makes the bottom of G(s) equal zero.
Poles are the roots of the denominator. They decide everything about stability and speed. Negative real part = the system settles. Positive = it blows up. On a graph they are drawn as ×.

Units: per second

zero A value of s that makes the TOP of G(s) equal zero.
Zeros shape the response (overshoot, undershoot) but they never decide stability. Drawn as ○. A zero on the right-hand side makes the output go the WRONG WAY first.

Units: per second

numerator The top part of a fraction.
In 3/4 the numerator is 3. In G(s) = 5/(s+2) the numerator is 5. Zeros come from the numerator.

Say it: NEW-mer-ay-tor

denominator The bottom part of a fraction.
In 3/4 the denominator is 4. In G(s) = 5/(s+2) it is (s+2). Poles come from the denominator, so the denominator is where all the stability action is.

Say it: de-NOM-in-ay-tor

characteristic equation The denominator of the closed loop, set equal to zero.
For a loop with open loop L(s), it is 1 + L(s) = 0. Solve it and you get the closed-loop poles. Almost every stability question in this course is secretly asking you to build this equation.
DC gain, G(0){\htmlClass{mc1}{G}}(0)G(0) What the output settles at for an input of 1. Just put s = 0 into G(s).
If G(0) = 2, then a step input of 1 eventually gives an output of 2. Quick and useful: it is the first thing to check when matching transfer functions to graphs.

Units: depends

strictly proper The bottom of the fraction has a higher power of s than the top.
e.g. 5/(s²+3s+2): top has s⁰, bottom has s². This means the output cannot jump instantly — the step response always starts at zero.
order The highest power of s in the denominator. Also: how many energy stores the system has.
First order = one store = PT1. Second order = two stores = PT2. Counting energy stores in the drawing tells you the order before you do any algebra — a great way to check your work.
dead time Tt{\htmlClass{mc1}{T}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st9}{t}}Tt​ A pure delay. Nothing happens at all for a while, then the response starts.
Caused by transport: a pipe, a conveyor belt, a slow sensor. Written e^(−Tₜs). It is the enemy of fast control because it eats phase margin without warning you on the magnitude plot.

Units: seconds

Describing a response

The numbers a specification is written in.

step response What the output does when you suddenly switch the input from 0 to 1 and leave it there.
Like flipping a light switch and filming what happens. It is the standard test because it is easy to do in the lab and it shows speed, overshoot and final value all at once.
impulse response What the output does after a single instantaneous kick.
Like hitting a bell once. Mathematically it is the inverse Laplace transform of G(s) itself. The step response is the running total (integral) of the impulse response.
1(t) (unit step) An input signal: zero before t = 0, one afterwards, for ever. Flipping a switch.
Written 1(t) in this course and σ(t) in many books; some write u(t), which collides badly with u for the input. It is the standard test because it is trivial to produce on real hardware and shows the gain, the speed and any overshoot on one trace. Integrate it and you get a ramp; differentiate it and you get δ(t).

Units: same as the input

Say it: the unit step

δ(t) (Dirac impulse) An input signal: one instantaneous kick, of zero width and area 1. The only δ in this course — the damping factor is ζ.
Think of a hammer blow. It is over before you can look, but it definitely did something — because what counts is its area, not its height. Feed it to a system and what comes back is the impulse response, which is the system with the input scrubbed out. Also: the derivative of a step is an impulse, so the impulse response is the derivative of the step response.

Units: area, in input units × seconds

Say it: di-RAK DEL-ta

PT1 A first-order lag. One energy store. Smooth rise, never overshoots.
G(s) = K/(Ts+1). The most common plant in the whole course. Examples: an RC circuit, a stirred heated tank, a tank draining through a valve.

Say it: pee-tee-one

PT2 A second-order system. Two energy stores. CAN overshoot and wobble.
G(s) = Kω0²/(s²+2ζω0s+ω0²). Examples: mass-spring-damper, RLC circuit, drone altitude. This is the single most examined element.

Say it: pee-tee-two

IT1 An integrator with a lag in front of it. Output ramps up forever.
G(s) = K/(s(Ts+1)). Typical of a motor driving a position: the motor has a lag, and position is the integral of speed.

Say it: eye-tee-one

τ (tau) The time constant: how long a first-order system takes to get 63 % of the way to its final value.
After 1τ you are 63 % there, after 3τ you are 95 % there, after 5τ you are 99 % there. A big τ means a slow, sluggish system. Sometimes written T instead.

Units: seconds

Say it: rhymes with "now" or "saw"

ω (omega) A frequency — how fast something wiggles, in radians per second.
Radians per second, not cycles per second. One full circle is 2π radians, so ω = 2πf where f is in Hz. If ω = 6.28 rad/s the thing does one full cycle per second.

Units: rad/s

Say it: OH-may-ga

ω₀ (omega-nought) The natural frequency: the rhythm a system wants to oscillate at if nothing damped it.
Like the pitch of a bell — set by the physical build of the thing. For a spring and mass, ω0 = √(K/M). For an RLC circuit, ω0 = 1/√(LC). Bigger ω0 = faster system.

Units: rad/s

Say it: OH-may-ga zero

ω_d (omega-d) The frequency you actually see wobbling, once damping has slowed it down a bit.
ωd = ω0√(1−ζ²). Always a little smaller than ω0, because friction slows the wobble down. If there is no damping (ζ=0) they are equal.

Units: rad/s

Say it: OH-may-ga dee

δ (delta) In your lecture slides and in the question text of your papers, the damping factor. This course writes that quantity as ζ.
Look it up under ζ — same quantity, and everything said there applies. The reason this app does not use δ for it is the other thing δ means: δ(t), the Dirac impulse, which is an input signal rather than a number describing the plant. One letter doing both jobs is a sentence you have to read twice. In the printed formula table your papers use a roman d for the damping factor as well, so you will meet all three letters; the exam uses whichever suits the line it is on.

Units: none (dimensionless)

Say it: DEL-ta

ζ (zeta) The damping factor, also called the damping ratio: how strongly friction kills the oscillation. A pure number with no units.
ζ = 0 means it rings forever. ζ = 1 is the exact boundary where wobbling stops being possible. ζ > 1 means it is too sluggish to wobble at all. ζ is what English textbooks write — Ogata, Nise — and it is what this course writes, on purpose: your lecture slides use δ for it, and δ is also the Dirac impulse δ(t), which is an input signal rather than a property of the plant. Your papers print a third letter, a roman d, in the standard form 1 + 2dT0s + T0²s² and in the step-response table, while the question text beside it asks for “the damping factor δ”. Three letters, one quantity: ζ, δ, d.

Units: none (dimensionless)

Say it: ZAY-ta

ϑ (theta, curly) A temperature measured as a rise above something else, rather than on an absolute scale.
In the heated-object lesson ϑ = T − Tamb, the rise above the room. Defining it that way is the whole trick of that lesson: the ambient term disappears from the model, and what is left is a plain PT₁ in ϑ. It is the same move as measuring a water level from the outlet rather than from the floor. German texts use this letter for temperature generally, which is why it turns up here rather than a plain T.

Units: K, or °C — a difference, so both are the same size

Say it: THAY-ta

Ω (ohm) The unit of resistance. A capital omega, and nothing to do with the lowercase ω that means a frequency.
One ohm is one volt per amp. It turns up here only as the unit on a resistance value, as in R = 2 Ω — but it is worth pointing at, because Ω and ω sit two lines apart in the RLC task and they are different quantities in different units. In the electrical-mechanical analogy the resistance is the damper: the thing that turns energy into heat rather than storing it.

Units: V/A

Say it: OHM

overshoot Mp{\htmlClass{mc2}{M}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st1}{p}}Mp​ How far past the target the output goes before coming back, usually as a percentage.
Mₚ = e^(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)). It depends ONLY on the damping ζ, never on ω0. Making a system faster does not make it overshoot more.

Units: % or fraction

peak time Tm{\htmlClass{mc1}{T}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st2}{m}}Tm​ How long until the output reaches its highest point.
Tₘ = π/(ω0√(1−ζ²)) = π/ωd. That is literally half a wobble, because the first peak happens half a period after you start.

Units: seconds

return time T5{\htmlClass{mc1}{T}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st5}{5}}%T5​ How long until the output stops straying more than 5 % from its final value.
T₅% = 3/(ζω0). Note ζω0 is exactly the real part of the pole, so this says: how fast a thing settles depends on how far left its pole sits.

Units: seconds

Stability

Three ways to ask one question, and the words the marker is looking for.

asymptotically stable Left alone, the system settles down to a steady value.
Happens exactly when EVERY pole has a strictly negative real part. "Asymptotically" just means "eventually, given enough time".
marginally stable It neither settles nor blows up — it wobbles forever at constant size.
Happens when poles sit exactly on the imaginary axis. In the exam always say "marginally stable", never just "stable" — the distinction is worth marks.
left half plane (LHP) The left side of the pole graph, where the real part is negative.
Poles here are good news: their modes die away like e^(−something×t). "All poles in the LHP" is the definition of a stable system.
right half plane (RHP) The right side of the pole graph, where the real part is positive.
A POLE here means the system blows up. A ZERO here is legal but makes the response go the wrong way first. Do not mix the two up.
Routh criterion A table that tells you if a system is stable WITHOUT finding the roots.
You build a triangle of numbers from the coefficients. Count how many times the first column changes sign — that is exactly how many unstable poles you have. Zero sign changes = stable.

Say it: ROWTH (rhymes with mouth)

Nyquist criterion A stability test done by drawing G(jω) as a curve in the complex plane.
For an open-loop-stable system: if the curve does not loop around the point −1, the closed loop is stable. The point −1 matters because 1 + L = 0 exactly there.

Say it: NYE-kwist

phase / gain margin How much room you have left before the loop becomes unstable.
Phase margin: how many extra degrees of lag you can take (want 40-60°). Gain margin: how many times bigger you could make the gain (want more than 2).

Units: degrees / factor

φ (phi) An angle, usually a phase angle in degrees.
φR is the phase margin — how many degrees of extra lag your control loop can take before it goes unstable. You want 40° to 60°.

Units: degrees

Say it: FYE or FEE

∠ (angle) The phase of a complex number or a transfer function: the angle its arrow makes, measured from the positive real axis.
∠G(jω) is how far the output lags the input at that frequency, as an angle. It is the second half of what a frequency response says — the first half is the magnitude |G(jω)|, how much bigger or smaller the output is. The two rules worth knowing are that it adds where magnitudes multiply, ∠(G₁G₂) = ∠G₁ + ∠G₂, and subtracts where they divide. That is the whole reason Bode plots are drawn on a log scale: it turns both halves into addition. In the margin formula φR = 180° + ∠L(jωc), the angle sign is the operator and φR is the answer it produces.

Units: degrees, or radians

Say it: the angle of

|G| (magnitude) The size of a complex number: how far it is from the origin.
For a + jb it is √(a²+b²) — just Pythagoras. In frequency response, |G(jω)| is how much the system amplifies a sine wave at that frequency.

Units: depends

Say it: mod G

decibel (dB) A way of writing gain on a squashed scale: 20·log₁₀(gain).
Gain 1 = 0 dB. Gain 10 = 20 dB. Gain 0.1 = −20 dB. Used on Bode plots so that huge and tiny gains fit on one graph, and so that multiplying becomes adding.

Units: dB

Say it: DESS-i-bell

Modelling and linearising

The vocabulary of the first half, which the second half is built on.

balance equation accumulation = what comes in − what goes out.
Every model in this course starts here, with charge, momentum, energy or mass as the thing being balanced. Write it in words first, then in symbols.
constitutive law The physical rule for one component. Ohm's law, Hooke's law, Newton's law.
The balance says WHAT is conserved; the constitutive law says HOW each part behaves. There are really only three kinds: dissipating (R), storing by filling (C), storing by moving (I).

Say it: con-STIT-you-tiv

state variable Whatever sits inside the derivative — the things the system "remembers".
Water level, capacitor voltage, position AND velocity. One state per energy store. Count them and you know the order of the model in advance.

Units: depends

operating point The steady state you are working around: nothing is changing.
Found by setting every derivative to zero and solving. It is the reference from which all Δ deviations are measured, and it is why the constant term disappears when you linearise.

Units: depends

linearisation Replacing a curve with the straight line that touches it at one point.
Control theory only works on straight lines. So we pick an operating point, draw the tangent there, and use that instead. Valid only near that point.
Taylor expansion Approximating a curve near a point using its value plus its slope.
f(x) ≈ f(x₀) + slope × (x − x₀). That is all first-order Taylor is: "start where you are, then follow the tangent". Everything else in the series is thrown away.

Say it: TAY-lor

Δ (capital delta) Means "a small change in". Δh means "a small change in the height h".
Used constantly in linearisation. If h is the actual water level and h₀ is the level we linearised around, then Δh = h − h₀ — how far we have drifted from that reference point. Nothing more mysterious than that.

Units: same as the variable it is attached to

Say it: DEL-ta

∂ (partial derivative) A derivative when there is more than one variable, and you change only ONE of them.
Flow out of a tank depends on both the level h and the valve setting u. ∂f/∂h means "how much does f change when I nudge h and keep u frozen". Used in every linearisation.

Say it: "partial dee" or "curly dee"

ẋ (a dot on top) The rate of change of x — how fast x is changing right now.
If x is a position in metres, ẋ is a speed in metres per second. It is just shorthand for dx/dt. Two dots (ẍ) means the rate of change of the rate of change — acceleration.

Units: x per second

Say it: "x dot"

′ (prime) A rate of change, written the other way. f′ means the same as the dot over a letter.
A dot is the convention when the thing being differentiated is time, which is nearly always the case here, so this course writes ẏ rather than y′. Prime turns up where the variable is not time — the slope of a curve f′(u∗) at an operating point, for instance, which is a rate of change with respect to u. Two primes means differentiate twice, exactly as two dots do.

Units: the quantity, per unit of whatever it is differentiated by

Say it: prime

d/dt The same thing as a dot: "how fast is this changing".
dh/dt is how fast the water level h is rising. If dh/dt = 0 the level is not moving — it is in steady state. That fact is used to find operating points.

Say it: "dee by dee tee"

Torricelli’s law Water squirts out of a hole at speed √(2gh).
Comes from energy conservation: the potential energy of the height h turns into kinetic energy of the jet. The square root is what makes tank models nonlinear.

Units: m/s

Say it: torri-CHELL-ee

State space

The same model, stacked into matrices.

matrix A grid of numbers, used to write several equations at once.
In state space, A is a grid holding all the coefficients. Its eigenvalues are the poles. You mostly just need to build it and take a determinant.

Say it: MAY-trix

eigenvalue A special number belonging to a matrix. For us: the same thing as a pole.
Found from det(sI − A) = 0. That determinant IS the characteristic polynomial, so the eigenvalues of A are exactly the poles of G(s). Two names for one set of numbers.

Units: per second

Say it: EYE-gen-value

determinant, det() One number squeezed out of a square grid of numbers.
For a 2×2 grid [a b; c d] it is simply ad − bc. That is all you need for this course.
λ (lambda) A characteristic root: a value that makes the homogeneous equation work with y = e^(λt).
Substitute e^(λt) into ÿ + 2ζω₀ẏ + ω₀²y = 0 and you get λ² + 2ζω₀λ + ω₀² = 0. Solve that quadratic and the two roots decide everything: real means exponential decay, complex means a wobble. These are the SAME numbers as the poles of G(s) - the time-domain name and the s-domain name for one set of values.

Units: per second

Say it: LAM-da

Reading the maths

Borrowed from maths rather than from control, and used in every line of it.

derivative A rate of change. The slope of a graph at one point.
On a distance-vs-time graph the derivative is your speed. On a temperature-vs-time graph it is how fast you are heating up. Steep graph = big derivative.
eee (Euler’s number) The number 2.71828..., the natural base for growth and decay.
e^(−t/T) is the shape of every decay in this course. At t = T it has fallen to 0.368 of its start, which is why the step response has reached 1 − 0.368 = 63.2 %.

Units: none

Say it: ee

· (centre dot) Also means multiply.
Used to separate things that would look glued together otherwise, e.g. 4·K(u*)³. It never means anything different from ×.

Say it: times

coefficient The number multiplying a symbol.
In 5s² the coefficient is 5. In as²+bs+c the coefficients are a, b and c. Routh works purely on coefficients, never on roots.

Say it: co-eh-FISH-ent

polynomial A sum of powers, like s³ + 4s² + 21s + 34.
The denominator of every transfer function in this course is a polynomial in s. Its roots are the poles.

Say it: polly-NOH-mee-al

root A value that makes an expression equal zero.
The roots of s²+3s+2 are s = −1 and s = −2, because putting either in gives zero. Roots of the denominator = poles.
complex number A number with two parts: a normal part and an "imaginary" part written with j.
a + jb. Think of it as a point on a map: a is how far right, b is how far up. Poles are complex numbers, which is why we can draw them on a 2D plane.
α (alpha) Not a named quantity in this course. It is a stand-in: a constant whose value is given by the line that introduces it.
Two places use it, and they mean different things by it, which is the point. In the ringing derivation it is the ratio α = ζ/√(1−ζ²), a shorthand that makes one trigonometric identity fit on a line. In the nonlinearity lesson it is the coefficient of the cubic term in a progressive spring, Kx + αx³. Neither is a definition you should carry between pages: when you meet α, the line above or below it says what it is this time. That is what a Greek letter with no fixed meaning is for.

Units: whatever the quantity it stands for carries

Say it: AL-fa

θ (theta) An angle. Which angle depends on the picture, and every picture that uses it says.
Three of them in this course, and they are unrelated. On the s-plane, θ is the angle a pole makes with the negative real axis, and the damping factor is its cosine: ζ = cosθ, so a pole straight out to the left has θ = 0 and no ringing at all. On a pendulum it is the angle from hanging-down, the thing the model is written in. Inside an integral it is often just the dummy variable being integrated over, with no meaning beyond "the time we are adding up".

Units: radians, or degrees

Say it: THAY-ta

≡ (identically) Stronger than equals: true for every value, not solved at one.
The exam papers use it for an input that is held at a value for the whole problem — "for u ≡ 0" means the input is zero throughout, not that you should solve for where it happens to be zero. That is a gift when you meet it: it tells you to substitute rather than to work anything out. The same bar is used to introduce a definition, as in Z(s) ≡ V(s)/I(s), meaning "this is what the left-hand side is defined to be".

Say it: identically

° (degrees) The unit an angle is quoted in when it is not in radians.
Phase is quoted in degrees throughout this course — a phase margin of 45°, the −180° that marks the cliff edge — while the maths underneath runs in radians. 180° is π radians, so a phase margin quoted as an angle and a frequency quoted in rad/s are in different units and must not be mixed in one expression without converting.

Say it: degrees

Which convention the letters follow

There are two in circulation and this app commits to one of them, because that is the one your papers are printed in.

This app uses German exam notation, because that is what the past papers print: W setpoint, Y controlled variable, U manipulated variable, Z disturbance, E error, GR controller, GS plant. An English textbook will write r, y, u, d, e, C and P for the same seven things. The glossary gives both, so you can read either.

With one letter deliberately out of step. The damping factor is ζ here, which is the English convention; your slides write it δ and the printed formula table writes a roman d. δ is already the Dirac impulse δ(t) in this course, and one letter that is both an input signal and a property of the plant is a line you have to read twice. Expect all three letters in the exam and read them as one quantity.
