The same statement, on the s-plane

Everything so far has a picture, and the picture is usually faster than the formulas.

δ{\htmlClass{mc1}{\delta}}δthe angle of the poles from the negative real axis: δ=cos⁡θ{\htmlClass{mc1}{\delta}} = \cos{\htmlClass{mc7}{\theta}}δ=cosθ ω0{\htmlClass{mc2}{\omega}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st0}{0}}ω0​their distance from the origin δω0{\htmlClass{mc1}{\delta}}{\htmlClass{mc2}{\omega}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st0}{0}}δω0​the real part — which is why settling time is 3/(δω0)3/({\htmlClass{mc1}{\delta}}{\htmlClass{mc2}{\omega}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st0}{0}})3/(δω0​) ωd{\htmlClass{mc2}{\omega}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st6}{d}}ωd​the imaginary part — the frequency you actually see wobbling

So sliding a pole outwards along a fixed ray makes the system faster and leaves its shape untouched; swinging it round towards the real axis calms it down and leaves its speed roughly alone. A specification of "at most 5 % overshoot, settled within 2 seconds" is therefore a region on the s-plane — a wedge from the angle condition, intersected with a half-plane from the real-part condition — and design is putting the closed-loop poles inside it.

T5%=3/(δω0){\htmlClass{mc4}{T}}_{5\%} = 3/({\htmlClass{mc1}{\delta}}{\htmlClass{mc2}{\omega}}_{\htmlClass{subtag st0}{0}})T5%​=3/(δω0​) is worth reading as "three over the distance the dominant pole sits to the left of the axis". Written that way it applies to any order, not just second, which is exactly how a fourth-order plant gets designed against a second-order specification.
